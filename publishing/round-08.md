### [Did Parkinson's law begin as a joke in *The Economist*?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-parkinsons-law-begin-as-a-joke-in-the-economist.html)

TRUE

**True.** Parkinson's law began as satire. The naval historian C. Northcote Parkinson opened a satirical essay in *The Economist* on 19 November 1955 with the "commonplace observation" that made his name: work "expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-parkinsons-law-begin-as-a-joke-in-the-economist.html)

Round 8 · No. 72

### [Was the Peter Principle written as satire?](https://conyso.com/publishing/was-the-peter-principle-written-as-satire.html)

TRUE

**True.** The Peter Principle was pitched as satire. The educator Laurence J. Peter and the playwright Raymond Hull set it out in their 1969 book: "In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence." It landed as serious management theory.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/was-the-peter-principle-written-as-satire.html)

Round 8 · No. 73

### [Was there a real committee that argued over a bike shed?](https://conyso.com/publishing/was-there-a-real-committee-that-argued-over-a-bike-shed.html)

FOLKLORE

**Folklore.** No real meeting lies behind the story. The reactor, the bicycle shed and the refreshment budget are C. Northcote Parkinson's invented example, from his 1957 book *Parkinson's Law, or The Pursuit of Progress*.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/was-there-a-real-committee-that-argued-over-a-bike-shed.html)

Round 8 · No. 74

### [Did Brooks admit his own law was an oversimplification?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-brooks-admit-his-own-law-was-an-oversimplification.html)

TRUE

**True.** Fred Brooks, who coined Brooks's law in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975), freely admitted that the law oversimplifies a real tendency: he introduced it with the words "Oversimplifying outrageously."

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-brooks-admit-his-own-law-was-an-oversimplification.html)

Round 8 · No. 75

### [Did Clarke add "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic" years later?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-clarke-add-any-sufficiently-advanced-technology-is-indistinguishable-from-magic-years-later.html)

TRUE

**True.** Arthur C. Clarke's most famous law came last. He stated his First and Second Laws in 1962 and added the Third, "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic," to the revised 1973 edition of the same book, having first published it in a 1968 letter to *Science*.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-clarke-add-any-sufficiently-advanced-technology-is-indistinguishable-from-magic-years-later.html)

Round 8 · No. 76

### [Has toast landing butter-side down been studied seriously?](https://conyso.com/publishing/has-toast-landing-butter-side-down-been-studied-seriously.html)

TRUE

**True.** In 1995 the physicist Robert Matthews published "Tumbling toast, Murphy's Law and the fundamental constants" in the *European Journal of Physics*. It won a 1996 Ig Nobel Prize, and it argued that toast does tend to land butter-side down, for mundane physical reasons.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/has-toast-landing-butter-side-down-been-studied-seriously.html)

Round 8 · No. 77

### [Did *Harvard Business Review* reject Conway's law?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-harvard-business-review-reject-conways-law.html)

TRUE

**True.** By Melvin Conway's own account, *Harvard Business Review* rejected his paper on the grounds that he had not proved his thesis. "How Do Committees Invent?" appeared instead in *Datamation* in April 1968.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-harvard-business-review-reject-conways-law.html)

Round 8 · No. 78

### [Did Philip Greenspun write rules one to nine?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-philip-greenspun-write-rules-one-to-nine.html)

FOLKLORE

**Folklore.** There are no rules one to nine. The "tenth" in Greenspun's Tenth Rule is itself the joke; Philip Greenspun has said he just wanted a memorable name.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-philip-greenspun-write-rules-one-to-nine.html)

Round 8 · No. 79

### [Did Kelly Johnson coin "Keep it simple, stupid"?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-kelly-johnson-coin-keep-it-simple-stupid.html)

DISPUTED

**Disputed.** The phrase is attributed to Kelly Johnson, lead engineer at Lockheed's Skunk Works, but the attribution is not airtight. A 1938 *Minneapolis Star* article used the variant "keep it short and simple," before Johnson's association with the phrase, and the earliest dated record of the acronym itself is a U.S. Navy "Project KISS" in 1960.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-kelly-johnson-coin-keep-it-simple-stupid.html)

Round 8 · No. 80

### [Did Fermi pose the Fermi paradox over lunch?](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-fermi-pose-the-fermi-paradox-over-lunch.html)

TRUE

**True.** The question began as a lunchtime remark by the physicist Enrico Fermi at Los Alamos in 1950, with his colleagues Teller, Konopinski and York. Firsthand accounts differ on his exact words, and the paradox's name and rigorous form came later, from others.

[The answer and its source →](https://conyso.com/publishing/did-fermi-pose-the-fermi-paradox-over-lunch.html)
