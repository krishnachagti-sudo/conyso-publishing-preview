### Did Archimedes shout "Eureka!" in the bath?

**Folklore.** Archimedes stated the law of buoyancy around 250 BC, in his treatise *On Floating Bodies*. The bath, the crown and the cry of "Eureka!" are almost certainly a later embellishment.

Tell someone: Archimedes did state the law of buoyancy, but the bath and the "Eureka!" almost certainly come from Vitruvius, writing some two centuries later.

### Did an apple fall on Newton's head?

**Folklore.** There is an apple in Newton's own account of his thinking about gravity. It does not hit him on the head. That detail is a later embellishment.

Tell someone: According to *Britannica*, Newton's own recollection includes an apple but no bump; the hit on the head was a later embellishment.

Cited in the book: Stukeley, *Memoirs of Sir Isaac Newton's Life* (1752). "Newton's law of gravitation," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Newton's law of universal gravitation," Wikipedia.

TRUE

### Did Newton himself tell the apple story?

**True.** The apple story is not a fable invented by Newton's admirers. According to *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, it traces back to Newton's own later recollection. Only the apple hitting his head was added afterward.

Tell someone: Newton himself told of the apple, according to *Britannica*; only the bump on the head was added later.

FOLKLORE

Round 1 · No. 4

### Did Galileo drop balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa?

**Folklore.** The tower drop is likely a legend. It is not in Galileo's own writings. His documented work on falling bodies used balls rolling down ramps, with notes from around 1603 to 1604 and results published in 1638.

Tell someone: The tower drop is likely a legend; Galileo's documented work on falling bodies rolled balls down ramps and timed them with a water clock.

Cited in the book: "Galileo's Leaning Tower of Pisa experiment," Wikipedia. "Two New Sciences," Wikipedia.

### Did Pythagoras discover the Pythagorean theorem?

**Folklore.** The relation that carries Pythagoras's name was known and used long before him. A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC lists its number triples, and Pythagoras lived from about 570 to 495 BC.

Tell someone: A Babylonian tablet from around 1800 BC already lists Pythagorean triples, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born.

- ["Pythagorean theorem," Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem)

Cited in the book: "Pythagorean theorem," *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. "Pythagorean theorem," Wikipedia.

Round 1 · No. 6

### Did Einstein write E = mc² in his 1905 paper?

**Folklore.** Einstein's 1905 paper contains the idea but not the famous formula. It states in a single sentence that a body which gives off energy as radiation loses mass in proportion.

Tell someone: Look for E = mc² in Einstein's 1905 paper and it isn't there: he set out the mass-energy relation in a sentence.

Cited in the book: Einstein, "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?" (1905), English translation. "Mass-energy equivalence," Wikipedia.

TRUE

Round 1 · No. 7

### Did Einstein win his Nobel Prize for something other than relativity?

**True.** Einstein's Nobel Prize, the physics prize designated for 1921, was for his work on the photoelectric effect. NobelPrize.org gives the citation as "for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect." Relativity is not what the prize named.

Tell someone: Einstein's Nobel was not for relativity: the prize designated for 1921 named his law of the photoelectric effect.

The book's full answer was checked on 12 November 2026 against

- [NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/summary/)
- [APS Physics 18, 12](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v18/12)
- [APS Historic Sites](https://www.aps.org/programs/honors/history/historicsites/millikan.cfm)

Cited in the book: Einstein, *Annalen der Physik* 322(6) (1905). The Nobel Prize in Physics 1921, NobelPrize.org. "Millikan's Measurement of Planck's Constant," APS Physics. "Robert Millikan," APS Historic Sites. "Photoelectric effect," Wikipedia.

### Did Gordon Moore say chips double every 18 months?

**Folklore.** Gordon Moore never gave 18 months. His 1965 article predicted that the number of components on a chip would double roughly every year, and in 1975 he revised that to every two years.

Tell someone: Gordon Moore predicted a doubling roughly every year in 1965 and every two years in 1975; the 18 months was David House's.

**Folklore.** In May 1940 Churchill offered "blood, toil, tears and sweat." That is four items, not three, and "toil" is the word that dropped out.

DISPUTED

Round 1 · No. 10

### Did Edward Murphy say "Anything that can go wrong will go wrong"?

**Disputed.** A maxim like it came out of an Air Force rocket-sled project around 1949, and Murphy's name stuck to it. Who first said it, and in what words, is not settled. The earliest firm printing under Murphy's name, from 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.

Tell someone: Who first said Murphy's law is disputed; its earliest firm printing under his name, in 1952, credits an unnamed physicist.

The book's full answer was checked on 12 November 2026 against

- ["Murphy's law," Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murphy%27s_law)

Cited in the book: Spark, *A History of Murphy's Law* (2006). "The Real-Life Murphy and How 'Murphy's Law' Came to Be," Military.com. Roe, *The Making of a Scientist* (1952).
